One cube, up close

Before counting every puzzle, hold a single one. A Rubik's Cube has six directions you can turn around — one per face. Draw each as an arrow from the centre. That bundle of arrows is the whole story.

The one thing to notice. Rotate the whole cube 90° about any one of its face axes and the six arrows shuffle among themselves. Not one of them ends up somewhere new — the bundle lands exactly back on itself. That is the test for a valid turning axis, and it is why the face alone can turn.

Or click any arrow in the view to rotate about it.

6 axes
where each arrow goes

Each turn is a permutation of the six arrows: the turning axis stays put, the four around it cycle, and the opposite one stays put too. Different turn, different shuffle — but always a shuffle of the same six.

Now break it. Add a seventh axis pointing wherever you like. Turn the cube — and watch where that new arrow lands. If it lands where no arrow was, the mechanism jams: a cut plane would run into the middle of a piece.
7 axes
—

The only fix is to add every arrow the turns send it to. Do that and the bundle closes up again — but notice the family is never size 7. It is 6, 8, 12, or 24. You cannot choose the size; the cube chooses it for you. That is why only finitely many bundles exist.
