Turning systems

A ray-set says which directions you can turn. A turning system adds how far: each ray gets a turn order — 4 = quarter turns, 3 = thirds, 2 = half turns only. The little polygon on each ray shows its order (a square = 4-fold, triangle = 3-fold, bar = half-turn).

Same rays, different puzzle. The cube's six face-axes carry three turning systems: all-4 is the 3×3×3, two pairs dialed to half-turns is the Domino, all-2 is a tetrahedral edge-turning cube. Only a 4-fold axis can be dialed down, and those live only on the cube faces — which is why just 4 of the 16 ray-sets have any freedom, giving 21 turning systems in all.

The two rules. Not every assignment of turn orders is legal. Closure: turning a ray by its step must land the whole ray-set back on itself. Constant on families: rays the generated group shuffles together must share a turn order. The Domino is legal because restricting two opposite face-pairs to half-turns is consistent; restricting only one is not — a 90° turn about one axis would carry it onto a half-turn axis.

Turn order isn't what your moves generate. Both turning systems on Cube faces + edges generate a group of order 24, yet they differ: in one, the face axes may only be half-turned, even though 90° rotations exist inside the generated group. What you're permitted to turn is not the same as what your moves reach.

Still not the whole puzzle. A Skewb and a Dino Cube are the same ray-set and the same turning system — yet different puzzles, because their cuts sit at different depths. That's the next refinement (stop #7).
